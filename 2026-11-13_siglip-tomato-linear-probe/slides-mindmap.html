Problem

Why should a computer judge ripeness at all?

  • Farmers still decide when to pick a tomato by looking at its colour.
  • Pick too early or too late and you lose money, and a harvesting robot has to get it right on its own.
  • Checking fruit by hand is slow, tiring work.
  • And expert labels don't come cheap.

Where it would help

  • Harvesting robots that only pick ripe fruit
  • Packing lines that sort tomatoes by ripeness
  • Quality checks as the fruit moves to market
So the real question is: how far can we get with no labels at all, and how much do a few labels add?

Source: Data in Brief (2023), PMC10618421

Say: "Ripeness is still judged by eye, mistakes cost money, and getting experts to label photos is expensive. That's what shaped our question: what can we do with no labels, and what do a few labels buy us?"

Research direction

One model, two ways of using it, compared fairly

  • Before: we describe each class in a sentence and let SigLIP pick the closest one. No training, no labels.
  • After: we keep SigLIP frozen and train one small layer on 651 labelled photos.
  • Same model, same photos, same test set, so any gap comes down to where the line between the classes gets drawn.
Our bet: SigLIP already "knows" what ripeness looks like. It just needs to be shown where the line is.

Where it could go next

  • Prompts that describe turning tomatoes, not just red ones
  • Letting SigLIP's last couple of layers learn too
  • Repeating with several random seeds to see how stable the numbers are

Say: "The research question is a comparison. Zero-shot is SigLIP using only words. The linear probe is SigLIP plus one tiny trained layer. Because everything else is held fixed, the difference between them tells us how much labelled examples add."

Data

Making sure the test is actually fair

  • Two classes: Immature and Mature.
  • The dataset also ships rotated and flipped copies of each photo. We left those out, or the test would contain pictures the model had effectively already seen.
  • Some photos were exact duplicates, so we removed them too, leaving 931.
  • 651 to learn from, 140 to check progress against, and 140 kept hidden until the very end.
  • Every group keeps the same mix: roughly 46% immature, 54% mature.

Say: "The main thing here is fairness. Augmented copies would leak test photos into training, so we used the originals only, removed duplicates, and split them so each group has the same balance."

Preprocessing

Making each photo look the way SigLIP expects

img.resize((224, 224), BICUBIC)   # shrink to 224×224
x = x / 255.0                      # 0–255 becomes 0–1
x = (x - 0.5) / 0.5                # now −1 to 1, SigLIP's range
x = x.permute(2, 0, 1)             # colour channels first
# stack into batches: B×3×224×224
We did this by hand and compared it with the official tool. The biggest difference anywhere was 0.0078, a single shade of brightness, so the two are effectively identical.

Say: "SigLIP was trained on 224-pixel images cut into 16-pixel squares, a 14 by 14 grid. We resize to match, scale the values to between minus one and one as SigLIP was trained, and put the colour channels first because that's what PyTorch expects."

The model

A model that understands pictures and words together

  • SigLIP has two halves: one reads images, the other reads text. Both turn what they see into 768 numbers.
  • A photo and a caption that match end up close together, which is why it can classify from a sentence.
  • Unlike CLIP, it learned by asking "do these two match?" one pair at a time, instead of picking the best caption out of a crowd.
  • Each photo gets boiled down to a single 768-number fingerprint, its pooler_output.
score = cos(image, text)   # how alike are they?
        × scale            # sharpen the difference
        + bias             # lean towards "no match"
The scale and bias are the same for both classes, so they never change which class wins.

Say: "SigLIP is from Google, 2023. Pictures and words live in the same space, which is the whole reason zero-shot works. Its twist on CLIP is judging each image–caption pair on its own, as a yes or no."

With only 651 photos, retraining 92.9 million weights would just memorise them. And since a frozen SigLIP gives the same fingerprint for a photo every time, we worked each one out once and trained on those. We checked: it's exactly the same as running the full model.

Say: "SigLIP never had a classification layer, so we added one small one on top and froze everything else. That's 1,538 numbers to learn instead of 93 million."

In a sense, the sentences are the classifier. Their two vectors do exactly the job a trained layer would.

Say: "Before any training, we just describe each class in words and let SigLIP pick the closest. With zero labels it gets 78.6% on the hidden test set. Not bad, but look where the mistakes land."

Training

Teaching the small layer, one batch at a time

We showed the layer all 651 photos 50 times over, 32 at a time, nudging its weights after every batch: about 1,050 small steps. The checking set liked epoch 49 best, and it was already right about 98.6% of the time after the first pass, so SigLIP's fingerprints separate the two classes almost straight away.

Say: "Both loss curves fall together and flatten out, and the best epoch is near the end, so it isn't overfitting. The striking part is how quickly it gets there."

Mistakes & limits

The tomatoes that words got wrong

Every one of zero-shot's 30 mistakes was a mature tomato it called immature. Look at them: yellow, orange, just starting to blush. Our prompt said "red", but the expert's idea of mature starts much earlier.
  • With 140 test photos, one photo is worth about 0.7 points, and we ran a single seed, so we don't read much into 99.3% versus 100%.
  • The trained layer's only miss (right) is a pale tomato the expert called immature, a genuinely borderline case.

Say: "SigLIP wasn't wrong about colour. Our words just drew the line at 'red', while the expert draws it earlier. Examples taught the layer where the expert's line actually is."
